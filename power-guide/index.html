Power the circuits you choose.

Use your home's normal lights and outlets through a compatible generator, inlet and transfer equipment. Choose what you need, within your generator's capacity and the limits of your installed system.

You don't have to run everything at once.

Cycling appliances lets you do more with the power you have. Give a larger appliance its turn, then switch it off before using another. Your installer helps you plan which loads can run together and which should take turns.

Think kitchen, comfort and home systems.

With enough power and compatible equipment, backup can extend to a microwave, furnace blower, well pump or A/C. Check actual running watts and the extra starting demand before counting on a larger load.

Considering A/C? A soft start may reduce startup demand. It does not add generator power or lower running watts. Have your equipment checked first.

Regular or inverter?

Two ways to make power. A few useful differences.

Conventional

The familiar "regular" generator

Often a lower-cost route to substantial output. Many use a steady-speed engine and an open frame. Check the exact model's noise, fuel use and power-quality specifications.

Inverter

Electronics control the output

Often lower THD (harmonic distortion) and steadier output, helpful for sensitive electronics. Check the model's THD and your device's guidance. Many adjust engine speed to save fuel and reduce noise. Check the exact model; not every inverter provides 240V.

The case is only part of the story.

Open-frame means the machinery sits inside an exposed protective frame. Enclosed means it has an outer housing. Inverters come in both styles.

Pick the fuel that fits your life.

Fuel choices and inverter technology are separate decisions.

Gasoline

One fuel. A straightforward starting point.

Plan safe fuel storage and follow the manual's storage instructions. Shut down and let the generator cool before refueling.

Dual fuel

Usually gasoline or propane. More flexibility.

A second fuel gives you options when one is hard to find. Keep propane cylinders upright and outdoors, away from enclosed spaces.

Tri fuel

Supports gasoline, propane and natural gas.

Choose one fuel at a time, following the manual. Natural gas can reduce refueling trips. A qualified professional must confirm the supply and connection; gas service can be interrupted.

Check power on the fuel you will actually use.

Running output often drops on propane or natural gas. Follow the manual for fuel selection and switching. Dual-fuel and tri-fuel portables still need safe outdoor operation.

Check the shape, the label and the voltage.

Look for actual 120/240V output.

The L14-20R has a different 20A connection. The TT-30R is 120V only. Three-slot twist-locks have different voltage configurations; check the NEMA marking. An adapter does not create missing 120/240V output or increase capacity. Have any other connection checked.

The outlet is only the first check.

Outlet amps are a connection limit, not available generator watts. Your electrician checks output on your chosen fuel, transfer equipment, cord length and wire size. Long cords can lower voltage. Plan a safe spot at least 20 feet from homes and buildings.

Safe power starts here.

A few essentials. Every time you use your generator.

20 feet or more Exhaust points away from openings.

  • Outdoors only. Keep at least 20 feet from homes and buildings. Point exhaust away from doors, windows and vents. Never run in a home, garage or enclosure, even with doors open.

  • Use working battery-powered or battery-backed CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas. If an alarm sounds or anyone feels sick, dizzy or weak, get outside immediately, then call 911.

  • Never plug a generator into a household wall outlet. Have an electrician install transfer equipment that isolates utility power.

  • Keep it dry. Follow the manual for weather protection and outdoor-rated cords.

  • Shut down and let it cool before refueling.

CO shutoff does not replace safe placement or working CO alarms.
